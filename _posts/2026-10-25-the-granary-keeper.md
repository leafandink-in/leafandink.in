# The Granary Keeper

The royal granaries of Ratnapura held enough rice to feed the kingdom through a failed monsoon, and that year the king needed somebody to keep them. It was an important post. The keeper had to count the grain, watch over the storehouses, and make sure every village received its share in times of drought.

Many young men came to the palace hoping to be chosen. Most belonged to well-known families; their fathers had served the king as ministers, or traded, or held land. Among them stood a young man named Gopal, the son of a potter from a small village beyond the river. He had never entered the palace before.

When the others saw his simple cotton clothes, some laughed. "Have you come to deliver water pots?" one of them asked.

"I have come for the king's test," Gopal replied.

The man laughed again. "The royal granaries are not a village clay shop."

Gopal said nothing.

Soon the chief minister entered the hall. "The king will choose the one who solves three problems," he announced, and he led them to the first granary. Inside were hundreds of sacks of rice. "One sack is spoiled," said the minister. "You may not open every sack. Find it before sunset."

The young men argued about it. One of them wanted every tenth sack opened. Gopal walked slowly between the sacks. He noticed a thin trail of ants disappearing behind one pile. He moved the sacks aside and found one that was damp underneath. The rice inside had begun to rot.

The minister nodded.

The next morning, for the second test, they were taken to another storehouse, where a munshi showed them a long record of grain received from the nearby villages. "Something is wrong with these accounts," he said.

Several of the young men began adding the numbers again and again. Gopal looked at the sacks instead. Ten of them, marked as wheat, were smaller than the rest. He lifted one in both hands, the way his father tested a pot fresh from the kiln.

It was far too light.

"There is nothing wrong with the adding," Gopal said. "But these sacks came in three-quarters full, and they went into the book as full ones."

The munshi started to say something about the carters, and stopped. The minister nodded, and before dark the munshi had been taken away to be questioned.

On the third day came the final test, and the king himself appeared.

"There are forty sacks in this granary," he said. "Pedapalli has asked for thirty and Konur has asked for thirty, and neither of them has had rain for three weeks. What do you send?"

One young man answered at once. "All forty to Pedapalli. It is the larger village."

Another said, "Thirty to the one that pays the higher tax, and the ten that are left to the other."

A third said he would send to the palace and ask.

Gopal thought for a moment. "I would send nothing at all until I knew what each of them still had in its own jars. Then I would divide the forty by what they have left and not by what they have asked for, and I would send word the same day to the two nearest granaries."

"And if the rule says the grain must go to one village only?"

"Then I would divide the forty anyway, and come and tell Your Majesty what I had done. I would rather be punished for that than let a village go hungry by the rule."

The hall became silent. The king smiled.

That evening, Gopal was appointed keeper of the royal granaries. One of the noblemen protested. "Your Majesty, his family has never served in the palace."

"That is true," said the king.

"He has no powerful relatives."

"That is also true."

"Then why choose him?"

The king looked at the ten light sacks still stacked by the storehouse door. "Because the grain does not care who his father is."

Years later, when the king needed a keeper for the granary at the river crossing, it was Gopal who set the test.
