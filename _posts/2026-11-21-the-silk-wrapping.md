# The Silk Wrapping

The king in Malini’s story was about to open the forbidden door when a boy in the audience shouted, “Do not!”

Malini stopped unrolling her painted scroll. “Shall I warn him?” she whispered.

“Yes!” cried the children.

From the front row, her friend Chandra shook her head so gravely that Malini nearly laughed. “I am afraid he will not listen,” Malini said. “This king has never listened to anyone in his life.”

Chandra always knew how to help her begin. Years ago, when Malini had forgotten her words in front of a crowd, Chandra had called, “And what happened next?” as though she simply could not wait to hear. The words had returned, and Malini had finished her story.

Now Malini travelled from village to village, performing in courtyards and festival grounds. Chandra often went with her. They shared dusty journeys, laughed over badly cooked dinners, and sometimes stayed in grand houses where neither of them quite knew which bowl was meant for washing their fingers.

Their friend Shanta stayed in Vidyanagar, making baskets. Whenever Malini came home, Shanta asked whether she had eaten, whether her sandals were holding, and whether she needed thread for her scrolls. Malini would answer quickly before telling her how many people had come to hear her.

One autumn, Malini was invited to the largest festival in the region. When she arrived, the festival head gave her a room beside the courtyard.

“You will perform on the final evening,” he told her. “That is when we have the largest crowd.”

“The final evening!” Chandra said when he had gone. “You must tell the story of the forbidden door.”

Nearby, a musician named Keshav was tightening the ropes on his drum. He had given the closing performance for the past two years. Malini turned to greet him, but he bent over his work.

Her travelling bag caught on the edge of a bench as she lifted it. Keshav looked up. “Leave that,” he said. “I will carry it.”

She thanked him and went to speak to the festival head about her performance. Keshav took the bag to her room, following a boy who was carrying her water jug.

That evening, visitors crowded around the festival’s famous silver box. Two elephants stood on its lid, their trunks curled together. Malini waited until a little girl had finished looking before stepping closer herself.

When the courtyard emptied, the keeper wrapped the box in blue silk embroidered with silver birds. He lifted it with both hands and carried it into the room behind the stage, then locked the door.

By morning, the lock was broken and the box was gone.

The festival head ordered the gates shut. Guards stood beside the waiting carts while other men began searching the rooms around the courtyard. Malini watched them shake out blankets and open travelling bags. Then one of the men came out of her room carrying a folded piece of blue silk.

She recognised the silver birds before he spoke.

“This was beneath your clothes.”

Malini stared at him. “That cannot have been in my bag.”

“Come and see.”

She followed him inside. Her spare clothes lay on the bed, her bag open beside them. She searched it herself, though she did not know what she expected to find.

“I did not put it there,” she said.

The festival head questioned her all morning. Where had she gone that evening? Had she been behind the stage? Had anyone seen her return to her room? She answered until her throat hurt, but each answer seemed to lead to the same question.

“Then how did the silk get into your bag?”

“I do not know.”

By noon, her name had been rubbed off the board announcing the final performance.

She found Chandra beside a sweet stall and caught her by the arm. “Come and sit with me,” she said.

Chandra put down the sweet she was holding. “They have taken your name off the board.”

“I know. Just sit beside me.”

Two women at the next stall stopped talking. Chandra glanced at them, then gently slipped her arm from Malini’s hand.

“I will come when there are fewer people,” she whispered.

Malini went back to her room. Each time footsteps sounded in the yard, she looked up. But the sellers packed their trays, the shadows reached her doorstep, and still Chandra did not come.

Near sunset, Shanta walked through the courtyard with a basket on her hip. Malini stood so quickly that she knocked against the door.

“How did you know?”

“A trader came back to Vidyanagar at midday.”

“Then everyone at home knows too.”

Shanta set down her basket. Malini tried to explain, but the words tangled together. She had answered questions all morning without crying. Now she could hardly speak.

Shanta sat beside her on the step and waited. After a while, she uncovered the basket. Inside were flatbreads wrapped in a cloth and two small oranges.

“Have you eaten?”

Malini shook her head.

“Neither have I,” Shanta said, breaking a flatbread in two.

They ate as the courtyard grew quiet. Malini told her about the search and the silk. She told her about Chandra too, though she kept her eyes on the orange she was peeling.

When she had finished, Shanta brushed the crumbs from her lap. “Who went into your room before the search?”

“The boy with the water. Keshav carried my bag.”

“Did anyone else?”

“Not that I saw.”

“Then tomorrow we will ask those two.”

They found the water boy early the next morning, filling pitchers at the well. He remembered showing Keshav the room and setting the jug beside Malini’s bed.

“Did you see him again afterwards?” Shanta asked.

The boy thought for a moment. “Before breakfast yesterday. He asked me to carry something to his cart.”

“What was it?”

“A rolled sleeping mat. He said there was a broken instrument inside and I must not drop it.”

“Was it heavy?” Malini asked.

“Oh, yes. I needed both arms.”

Malini turned to Shanta. The silver box had taken both the keeper’s hands to lift.

They brought the boy to the festival head. He listened, then called a guard to accompany them to the carts. The guards had watched the carts since the theft, but the search had not reached them yet.

The boy led them to a cart with one red wheel. A rolled mat lay behind a grain sack.

As the guard reached for it, Keshav hurried across the yard. “What are you doing with my things?”

“The boy carried this for you,” the festival head said. “We need to look inside.”

“It is only an instrument.”

“Then help us unwrap it.”

Keshav did not move.

The guard lowered the bundle to the ground. Something struck the stones with a hard clunk. When he loosened the rope, the mat opened, and the two silver elephants gleamed in the morning light.

The boy backed away. “I did not know,” he said. “He told me it was broken. I did not look.”

“No one is blaming you,” Shanta said, putting a hand on his shoulder.

Keshav stared at the box. At last he admitted that he had broken the lock in the night. He had hidden the box in the mat, and gone back in the dark to put its wrapping among her clothes.

“Two years I have closed this festival,” he said. “Next year nobody will send for me.”

Malini remembered the hours in her room, listening for footsteps. “You heard them questioning me,” she said.

Keshav lowered his head.

That afternoon, the festival head stood before the crowd and explained what had happened. He apologised to Malini, then wrote her name back on the board himself.

People who had turned away from her now came forward to speak. Malini answered politely, but she was tired. She wanted a little quiet before she told her story.

Chandra found her in her room, winding the cord around her scroll.

“I should have come yesterday,” she said.

Malini kept her eyes on the cord. “Yes.”

“I was frightened they would suspect me too. Everyone was watching.”

“I know they were.”

Chandra sat on the edge of the bed. For once, she seemed unable to think of anything to say.

“I kept looking for you,” Malini said.

“I am sorry.”

Malini looked at her then. She was glad Chandra had said it, but the hurt did not disappear. Outside, the drums sounded, calling people to the evening performance. She picked up her scroll.

“I have to go.”

Through the curtain beside the stage, she saw Shanta standing near the back of the crowd, her basket held against her knees. Malini asked the water boy to bring her forward.

“There is a place here,” she told Shanta.

“I can hear perfectly well from the back.”

“I know. I would like you here.”

Shanta settled into the front row. Malini loosened the cord around her scroll, took a breath, and looked out at the waiting children.

“There was once a king,” she began, “who had been told never to open a certain door.”

A boy near the front threw up his hands. “He is going to open it!”

Malini smiled. “Would you?”

The boy hesitated. “What is behind it?”

“That,” said Malini, “is exactly what the king wanted to know.”

The next morning, Shanta came to say goodbye. She had baskets to finish in Vidyanagar and could not stay another day.

As Shanta lifted her basket, Malini noticed the handle pulling loose. Shanta had wrapped a strip of cloth around it, but the broken reed still pressed into her palm.

“Was it like that when you came?”

Shanta nodded.

“You carried our food all that way with a broken handle?”

“It still holds.”

Malini reached for the basket. “Give it to me.”

“You have your own packing.”

“It can wait.” She fetched the strong thread she used for her scrolls and sat on the step. “I may need you to show me how.”

Shanta sat beside her and held the handle steady while Malini began.
