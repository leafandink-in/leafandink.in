# What Do You Think

Long ago, in the town of Kanchipuram, there was a small workshop that made painted clay lamps. It belonged to a woman named Ambika, and eight people worked in it.

Ambika could do most of the work herself. What she did instead was ask.

When a batch came out of the kiln cracked, she did not tell old Perumal, who had charge of the fire, what he should change. She said, "What do you think happened?"

If a design stopped selling, she went to Alamelu, who painted, and the question was always the same one: which colours were people asking for now.

The two sisters who packed the lamps in straw came to her about the ones that arrived broken. "How should we pack them?" she said.

The other workshop owners in the street thought this was a strange way to run anything. But after a while her people stopped waiting to be asked. Alamelu came in one morning with a mango-leaf border she had worked out, and it sold better than anything they had made. Perumal rearranged the way the lamps stood inside the kiln, and the cracking almost stopped.

Ambika never let either of those be called hers. She said whose they were, to customers, to the owners in the street, to anyone who asked.

Then, a fortnight before Deepavali, the rain came through the roof.

It came through at the north end, over the racks where four hundred lamps stood drying unfired. Some went to mud where they stood. The rest cracked as they dried crooked.

Their largest order of the year was due in six days.

Ambika stood looking at the racks for a long time. Then she called everyone together.

"I will go to the customer tomorrow," she said. "We may have to give back the advance."

Nobody said anything. Then Perumal said, "Why are you deciding already?"

Ambika looked at him.

"We have lost four hundred lamps."

"Yes," he said. "And we still have six days."

Alamelu said, "We can leave the borders off the small ones. Nobody counts the small ones."

Thangam said the sisters would pack through the night once there were lamps to pack.

Muthu, who threw the clay, knew two potters in the next street with moulds standing idle. He would go before they opened.

Then Kannan, who ground the colours and was the youngest person there, said the grain shed next door was empty and dry, and the racks could be in it by evening.

Then they were all talking at once.

Ambika let it run. Then she said, "All right. Tell me what each of you needs."

For six days that workshop was busier than it had ever been, and nobody waited to be told the small things. Perumal fired day and night and slept beside the kiln. Alamelu set the painters in a line and had the small lamps going out plain. Muthu had the borrowed moulds working by noon of the first day, and the sisters repacked so that a basket could be carried by one person instead of two. Ambika worked wherever there was a gap.

On the sixth evening the last baskets went up onto the cart. The order was complete.

They stood in the yard a while and watched the cart get smaller.

The roof was still open to the sky at the north end. Ambika looked up at it.

"Now," she said. "The roof. What do you think?"

Four people answered at once.
