# The Hill Road

Long ago, in the kingdom of Chandragiri, a young merchant named Nanda took over his father's trade. His father had died in the spring, and the drivers and the loaders and the men in the yard all waited to see whether the son could keep it going.

Then an order came from the palace. Fifty carts of rice, to reach the capital before the monsoon closed the roads. If the rice got there, the palace would buy from his house every year after.

Nanda had twenty carts.

Sridhar had thirty. His family was the richest in the town and liked everybody to know it, and he heard Nanda out with his hands behind his back.

"You can have my thirty carts and my cartmen," he said. "But when they come through the palace gates, people are going to say it was mine as much as yours."

Nanda thought about it for a day. Nobody else in the town had thirty carts standing idle, and he had eleven days.

"Agreed."

Two others went with them. Ramu had been his friend since they were boys and could talk to anybody. "Nothing can go wrong while I am with you," he said. Ramu said things like that often. The other was Hari, who had driven carts for Nanda's father for twenty-two years and hardly said anything at all.

Four days out, the road split in two. The river road ran flat and straight. The hill road went up and round the long way and cost a day and a half. All morning there had been cloud sitting on the hills to the west.

Hari came and stood beside Nanda. "The hill road."

"It is a day and a half longer."

"If it rains up there, the river comes over the road at the crossing. Then we sit on the bank and wait for it to go down. Could be a day. Could be a week."

Sridhar had come up behind them. "My cartmen do not use the hill road."

"It is a cart road," said Hari.

"It is a cart road for empty carts. Mine are loaded." Sridhar looked at Nanda. "And they are mine. They go by the river."

So there it was. The hill road, and Sridhar takes his thirty carts home that afternoon. Or the river road, and all fifty stay together.

"The river road," said Nanda.

It rained in the hills that night, and it rained all the next day. On the second morning the water was over the road a mile short of the crossing, and ten carts were in it up to the hubs before anybody understood how soft the ground had gone.

It took a day and a night to get them out. Men went in to their waists and put their shoulders under the axles. Two days were lost.

Nanda stayed up with the grain. He and Hari and two of the old drivers lifted the top sacks off the worst of the carts and carried them to higher ground, and when Nanda pushed his hand down into the lower sacks the rice was wet through and warm.

In the morning he heard his own name outside and came out.

Down the line, Sridhar's cartmen were unhitching the bullocks. One cart had already been turned round in the road. While Nanda stood watching, a second swung out of the line and came round to face the way they had come.

He went down the line fast. "What is this?"

"We are going home," said Sridhar.

"We have four days."

"You have four days." Sridhar was checking the strap on his saddle. "I am not having my name come through the palace gates on a delivery that is late."

"You promised me thirty carts."

"I promised you my carts. You have had my carts for six days." He got up on his horse. "I have not taken one thing from you that was yours."

He rode out, and the thirty carts went after him one behind another, all the way back down the road, until Nanda could not hear them any more.

Ramu was very upset about it and said so for a long time. Then he said the thing to do was to get word to the palace before anybody else did and explain, and that he was the man to do it, because he knew how to talk to people like that.

"Go, then," said Nanda.

Ramu took a horse and went, and Nanda did not see him again until the whole thing was over.

That left twenty carts, four days, and more wet rice than dry.

Hari was at the back of the nearest cart with his hand flat on a sack, feeling for the damp.

"Can it be done?"

"Some of it."

He took a bullock and rode off down the road and did not come back until dark, and when he came back he did not say where he had been.

Nanda slept badly and woke before light.

There was a sound coming up the road. He went out to the edge of the camp and stood in the dark and listened to it, and then he saw them. Carts, one behind another, coming up through the trees with lamps swinging under the axles. He counted ten. He was still counting when the last of them came out of the trees.

Thirty carts came into the camp that morning, and the men on them were men who had driven for his father. Nobody wrote anything down. Nobody asked him for a figure. They pulled in, and turned round, and started loading the dry sacks.

"Why?" Nanda said to the oldest of them.

The man looked over at Hari, and then back at Nanda. "Your father paid us in a bad year when he did not have it," he said. "And once Hari told your father that crossing would go under, and your father turned the carts around."

Nanda turned round, but Hari had gone off to see to the bullocks.

The dry rice went up the hill road that morning, and the new carts came down and took what was left. Hari rode to a farmer he knew who still had rice in his yard from the last harvest, and the farmer sold it to them for what it was worth and not a coin more. The spoiled sacks were left behind.

On the last morning the final cart came up to the palace gates. The king's man walked down the line and counted.

"Not one day late."

Sridhar came to the house when they were home. He was very warm about it and said he had never doubted it for a moment. Ramu came too, with a long account of the useful things he had said in the capital. Nanda gave them both tea.

When the next order came from the palace, he did not open it in the house. He carried it down to the cart yard, and sat on a cart shaft with the paper on his knee, and read it out to Hari.
