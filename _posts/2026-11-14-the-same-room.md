Long ago, on the road between Ujjain and the western ports, there stood a small travellers' rest house. Merchants, pilgrims and scholars stopped there on their journeys.

The dharmashala belonged to an elderly man named Keshav, and his daughter Leela helped him run it. The building was nothing much: twelve rooms, a courtyard, a stable for bullocks and horses, and a large neem tree near the well.

Yet travellers came back. Leela knew almost everyone who had stayed there before.

One evening a merchant walked into the courtyard. Before he could speak, Leela smiled. "Welcome back, Jagannathji. Is your knee troubling you again?"

The merchant laughed. "You remember?"

"Last time you asked for a room downstairs, because the steps hurt." And she gave him that same room.

A little later, a family from the south arrived. Leela knew the youngest girl at once. "Meena! Did your parrot ever learn to say your name?"

The girl's eyes widened. "He says it all day now!"

That night an old pilgrim came, and Leela set a small bowl of curd beside his meal. The pilgrim looked at the bowl for a moment, and then went on with his meal without saying anything about it.

Her father had watched all this from his seat by the door. "You have a strange memory," he said afterwards.

"He told me about the curd himself," said Leela. "He was standing where you are standing."

A few years later Keshav died, and Leela took charge of the dharmashala.

One afternoon that same season a tired young man arrived alone. Leela looked at him for a while. She had seen him once, almost two years before.

"You travelled with an older man last time," she said.

The young man was quiet. "My father."

"He was going to Nashik to pray for your sister. She had been very ill."

He stared at her. "You remember my sister?"

"Her name was Tara, was it not?"

For a moment he could not speak. Then he smiled. "She recovered."

"I am glad."

"My father died last winter," he said.

Leela said nothing for a little while. Then she brought him water and showed him to the same room where he and his father had stayed.

That evening the young man sat under the neem tree until it was dark. He left before first light, and Leela did not see him go.

Not long after that, a wealthy man built a much larger rest house further down the road. It had painted walls and brass lamps, and a stable twice the size of hers.

The village was worried for her. "Travellers will go there now."

"Some will," said Leela, and went back to drawing water.

For several months, many travellers did go to the grand rest house. Then, one monsoon evening, a caravan halted at Leela's gate. At its head was Jagannath, the merchant with the bad knee.

"You passed the new rest house," Leela said. "It is much larger than mine."

Jagannath climbed down slowly from his cart. "Yes. When I stayed there last month, they gave me a room on the upper floor."

Leela pointed towards the downstairs room. "It is ready."

Others came back. The horse trader led his grey mare down to the far end of the stable himself, which was where Leela had always put her, because the mare kicked. The scholar found a lamp already burning outside his door and did not ask who had lit it.

Years later a cart came in at dusk with a young woman driving it and an old man asleep in the back under a blanket. Leela came out with a lamp and held it up and looked at him for a while.

"Take the downstairs room," she said. "Your father cannot manage the steps."
